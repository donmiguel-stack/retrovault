The Vault

Your childhood shelf, rebuilt like a modern game library.

Steam made it normal to browse your games as a wall of box art — searchable, filterable, one click from playing. The machines that invented home gaming never got that treatment. Retro Vault gives it to them: a library shell that runs entirely on your own computer, in your browser, offline if you like, and treats a 1979 cartridge with the same care Steam gives a new release.

Every game gets a real page: original box art, screenshots, publisher, year, genre, player count — and where history left something behind, the Vault keeps it. Hidden cheat write-ups sourced from the community. Homebrew spotlights for the developers still making games for these machines today, with links to support them. A featured rail, favorites, and per-shelf search that make nearly 400 releases feel browsable instead of archaeological.

Everything plays straight in the browser, no installs — the built-in emulators (O2EM, VICE, vAmigaWeb and DOSBox, compiled to WebAssembly) do the work the moment you drop a file into the right folder.

1981 – 1982 · The Videopac Museum Pieces

The Master Strategy Series

In 1981, designers Ron Bradford and Steve Lehner — with Ed Averett, the man behind most of the Odyssey² catalogue, doing the programming — tried something nobody else was doing: games that were half video game, half board game. Each box held a full game board, decks of tokens and a rulebook, and the console handled only what consoles were good at — the action, the arithmetic, the referee. Three shipped before Philips left the market. A fourth, a Sherlock Holmes game, was underway when the lights went out and was never finished.

The Vault gives the series its own exhibit on the Videopac shelf: the real board photographed for each game, what shipped in the box, and gameplay footage.

Quest for the Rings

1981
Most Innovative Game of 1981 — Electronic Games

Ten rings scattered through dungeons and shifting halls. Pick Warrior, Wizard, Phantom or Changeling and race another player to find them first — the board, overlay and forty-plus tokens turn the TV into just one part of the table.

The game board

Conquest of the World

1982
Honorable mention, Best Multi-Player Game of 1982 — Electronic Games

Risk with a video game instead of dice. Forty-three world powers, each rated for military and economic strength — and every battle is settled by playing the cartridge's own combat game rather than rolling anything.

The game board

The Great Wall Street Fortune Hunt

1982
Most Innovative Game of 1982 — Electronic Games

Twenty-seven real companies and commodities — IBM, McDonald's, gold, bonds — with prices that move on the news and never run the same way twice. Buy low, sell high, end the year richer than up to three opponents.

The game board

All three live on the Vault's Videopac shelf with box contents, board photos and gameplay — the closest thing to opening the boxes again.

Still Being Made

Homebrew, alive and well

Every shelf in the Vault has the same thing going on underneath the classics: people are still writing brand-new games for these machines, decades after the manufacturers walked away. Retro Vault actively supports and promotes homebrew work — it's the reason Videopac, C64, Amiga and MS-DOS aren't just museum pieces, and every shelf ships its own rotating homebrew spotlight with gameplay clips and a direct link to the people making them.

FAQ

Questions people actually ask.

Do I need to install anything besides the Vault itself?+

No manual install needed — the ZIP includes a launcher: double-click Retro Vault.app on Mac (a real app with its own icon — drag it to your Desktop, Applications or the Dock) or Start Retro Vault.bat on Windows, and it takes care of Python for you, installing it automatically if it isn't already on your machine. First run only, you may see a one-time prompt: Mac — right-click the app → "Open" → "Open" (macOS is just confirming it trusts a downloaded file). Windows — click "More info" → "Run anyway" on the SmartScreen warning. After that it's a plain double-click. Prefer to install Python yourself first? Mac: open Terminal and run xcode-select --install. Windows: grab the installer from python.org and tick "Add python.exe to PATH" during setup.

What is Python, and why do I need it just to browse my games?+

Python is a programming language that happens to ship a built-in mini web server — the launcher (or python3 serve.py in the Vault folder, if you run it by hand) switches it on. Browsers block a lot of what the Vault needs — loading the WebAssembly emulator cores, fetching game data — when a page is opened straight from a file on disk. Serving the same files over http://localhost instead, even though it's still 100% local, is what makes the browser treat it like a normal website and let everything load.

Is "localhost" safe? Does anything leave my computer?+

Nothing leaves your machine. localhost just means "this computer talking to itself" — Python is serving files from your own hard drive to your own browser, over a connection that never touches the internet. No games, saves or files are uploaded anywhere.

Which browsers and operating systems does it run on?+

Chrome is the one we'd pick. Firefox works fine too, just with one extra click before a game starts. We'd steer clear of Safari — it clears stored site data after about a week of no visits, and that's exactly where your favourites and save games live. Any of them will run on Windows, macOS or Linux — anything modern enough for WebAssembly. No installs beyond Python, no plugins.

Can I just play, without downloading anything?+

Yes. The live demo is the real Vault, running on our own server — pick a shelf, click a cover, play. Out-of-print titles load their game files from us; the rest are on the shelf but stay locked until you add your own. Downloading the Vault gives you the same thing offline, plus your own files, your own favourites, and save games that stay on your machine.

Do I need to already own the original games?+

Not for most of them any more. Retro Vault hosts the out-of-print titles itself — over 300 Videopac, C64, Amiga and MS-DOS games that nobody sells today start with nothing to add, in the demo and in your own install alike. Games that are still on sale are the other half: those the Vault will never carry, and you supply your own dump of a copy you own — the Resources page lists where people usually look. Homebrew and copyright-free releases ship with the Vault. See the License below for the legal details.

Do I need an Amiga Kickstart ROM?+

No. The Amiga shelf boots on AROS, the free, open-source Kickstart replacement that comes with the emulator, and every public-domain and homebrew title on the shelf was tested on it. A few commercial games insist on the real thing: if you own a Kickstart 1.3 image (Amiga Forever is the licensed way to get one), name it kick.rom, drop it into emulator/roms and the Vault picks it up by itself. We never ship or host a Commodore Kickstart.
